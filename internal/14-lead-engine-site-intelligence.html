01 · The Principle

Two rejected options, one principle.

The co-founders considered building a tool with the Google Maps API to map and monitor construction sites, and considered systematic scouting of mapped areas, location by location, with a vehicle and fuel budget. Both are examined here — and both are declined, for reasons worth writing down.

OptionThe honest assessmentVerdict
Build a Maps API tool now The Places API lists businesses that already exist — shops, offices, registered companies. It does not flag "under construction." A tool built today would produce a database of addresses that still has to be verified in person for stage, ownership and the people behind it. It automates the easy part — finding places — and none of the valuable part: knowing who is building, what stage they are at, and when they will order windows. Cost: developer time, API billing, maintenance. Benefit: none that Google Earth Pro doesn't give for free. Deferred — rebuild it as automation once the manual routine proves which corridors yield.
Drive and scout, area by area Blind scouting is the most expensive way to find sites: fuel spent on hope, and the yield depends on luck. The city is too large to patrol and most drives will pass nothing relevant. The resource waste the co-founders worried about is real. Rejected — replaced by targeted corridor days aimed by satellite.
This protocol A third way: free satellite change detection aims a small amount of driving at verified sites; the site board turns each visit into several named contacts; a dossier keeps the reach-out consistent until each site converts or dies. One tank a week, ceiling enforced. Adopted
The automation gate. The Maps API tool is not cancelled — it is postponed behind evidence. If, after 13 weeks, the corridor routine consistently yields conversations, the manual sheets become the specification and the tool gets built to automate exactly what worked. Automation follows proof; it never precedes it. This is the same discipline as the sprint's one-experiment rule.
02 · The Site Board

One visit, five leads.

Almost every serious construction site in Ghana carries a project signboard at its entrance — and that board is a lead list the competition is driving past. It typically names the people DS needs to know before a single window is ordered.

1
The developer / project owner
The one paying. Owns the decision on suppliers for owner-funded work, and usually owns more than one project — today and next year.
2
The main contractor
Controls procurement on contract work, orders materials in bulk, and works on multiple sites — each one a repeat opportunity.
3
The architect
Writes the specification — the single most valuable name on the board. Influenced before blockwork, locked after. DS has 20 years of reputation with this exact profession.
4–5
Consultants & the QS
Structural engineers, project managers, quantity surveyors. The QS prices the windows into the bill of quantities; the PM coordinates the trades. Each runs their own network of future sites.

Photographing one board therefore converts a single drive-by into four or five named, phone-numbered contacts — before any cold call has been made. And because the board also shows the project stage by simple observation (foundation, blockwork, roofing, fit-out), the approach can be timed to the moment windows actually get decided.

Why this matters more than any other lead source. A lead from an advert is a stranger. A lead from a site board is a named decision-maker attached to a real, dated, visible project — with a stage that tells DS exactly when to call. No ad budget in the country can produce that quality at this price.

How the map works

Fixed routes, repeatedEach corridor is driven as a fixed route once a month — the same road, the same way. Repetition is what makes it change detection: anything new stands out immediately.
Aim before drivingBefore each corridor day, satellite imagery flags which sections have new activity. The route may be shortened, extended or re-ordered — but it is never driven blind.
Corrected monthlyAt the monthly review, corridors are graded by yield: conversations and quotes per visit. Low-yield corridors are dropped or re-routed; high-yield ones are driven more often. The map earns its place on the scoreboard like everything else.
04 · Satellite Change Detection

Aim the vehicle. Free.

Google Earth Pro is free, runs on the office computer, and shows satellite imagery of every corridor in Ghana. Its historical slider — imagery from past months and years — is a change-detection tool that tells DS where new construction has appeared, before the vehicle leaves the yard.

Step 1 · DrawDraw the six corridor polygons once and save them. Each corridor day has its own folder of saved imagery views.
Step 2 · CompareOnce a month, per corridor: slide the historical imagery between "6–12 months ago" and "now." New roofs, new slabs and cleared land show clearly at residential and commercial scale.
Step 3 · MarkDrop a pin on every location where something new appeared. Export or note the pins for the week's route.
Step 4 · VerifyThe corridor day visits only the pinned locations — plus whatever is visible from the road in between. Fuel is spent on verified targets. Never on hope.

What the satellite cannot do — and who covers the gap

StageImagery may be weeks old. The corridor visit reads the actual stage on the ground — foundation, blockwork, roofing, fit-out — and the approach is timed accordingly (section 05).
NamesSatellite shows buildings, not people. The site board supplies the developer, contractor and architect. The two tools complete each other; neither is sufficient alone.
Speed of changeImagery updates lag by weeks in some areas. This is acceptable: the dossier keeps every discovered site in the loop regardless of when the satellite next refreshes.
The free stack, and what would be bought later. Google Earth Pro (free) + a phone camera + one shared sheet carries this protocol indefinitely. If the 13-week review proves the yield, the first paid upgrade is not the Maps API — it is a modest tool or script that automates imagery comparison and pin management for the proven corridors. Automation is bought for scale, after evidence, and priced against the fuel it saves.
05 · The Corridor Day

One day a week. One tank, ceiling.

One fixed corridor day per week — say Wednesday — replacing all ad-hoc site driving. Each day covers one corridor, in a route planned the previous evening from the satellite pins. The purpose of the day is not to sell on the spot; it is to photograph, stage-read and log every active site.

Before · the evening priorOpen Google Earth Pro, load the corridor, compare imagery, mark new pins, sequence them into one driving route. Print or screenshot the pin list.
On the route · at each sitePhotograph the site board in full. Photograph the stage (slab, blockwork, roof, fit-out). Log the location pin. Note security's openness — some boards are best photographed on the next pass; never argue at a gate.
Opportunity on the dayIf a contractor or site manager is present and receptive, one two-minute conversation: who DS is, the fast-cash menu, a card left. Anything deeper is booked for a follow-up call from the office — the corridor day stays a harvesting day.
Same eveningAll boards logged into the dossier: names, numbers, stage, pins, next action. An unlogged site did not happen — the same rule as the sprint's call log.
Cost ceilingOne fuel tank per corridor day. If a corridor cannot be covered within that, it is split across two weeks — the ceiling holds so the engine can never become the resource drain the co-founders feared.

The stage rules — when to strike

Foundation · slabWindows are months away, but the specification is being written now. Approach: the architect from the board. Purpose: get DS's system drawings and finishes into the spec before it is priced. Follow-up every 4 weeks.
Blockwork · roofingThe order window is opening — weeks, not months. Approach: the main contractor and developer directly, with a measured quotation fast-tracked. Follow-up every 2 weeks.
Fit-out · glazing stageBuying now, or already bought. Approach: urgent quote for remaining openings, plus repairs, balustrades and anything the winner missed. If the windows are already supplied — log who supplied them and be first on the next project.
Completed · occupiedRetrofit and maintenance leads: shower enclosures, repairs, next-phase work, and the developer's next project. The board of a finished site still names the whole team for the future.
06 · The Dossier

Monitor and reach out consistently.

The dossier is one shared sheet — the memory of the engine. Every site ever logged stays in it until it converts or is declared dead, and no site is ever approached inconsistently again. It is the sheet that turns "scouting" into a system.

ColumnWhat it holds
Site ID · date loggedSequential number and the date first seen. Weeks in the dossier become a live record of DS's market coverage.
Location · corridorGPS pin or landmark, and the corridor it came from — which grades the corridors at the monthly review.
Stage · date readFoundation / blockwork / roofing / fit-out / completed, with the date observed. Re-read at every pass; the stage date drives the strike timing.
The board namesDeveloper, main contractor, architect, consultants — each with a phone number, each a separate outreach row. One site can spawn up to five rows.
Contact logEvery call, visit, WhatsApp and response, dated — for each named contact. The reach-out cadence (2–4 weeks by stage) is enforced by the sheet, not by memory.
StatusActive → quoted → won · or dead after the kill rule: six documented attempts with no response, then archived without sentiment.

The cadence rules

Reach-out rhythmRoofing/fit-out sites: every 2 weeks until quoted or closed. Foundation sites: every 4 weeks, architect-first, until the spec stage passes.
Consistency ruleNo contact row goes past its due date. On Monday mornings Gifty prints the overdue list; Daniel clears it in the daily sales block before any new-name calling.
Feeding the sprintNamed contacts flow into List 5 of the Emergency Sales Strategy and are counted in its five numbers. The dossier is upstream; the scoreboard is downstream; nothing is double-counted.
Free harvest channelDrivers and installers photograph boards on routes they already travel — zero extra fuel. Their photos enter the same dossier with a "driver report" tag and earn a small acknowledged credit.
07 · The Permit Feed

And the specifier door.

Two more channels complete the engine. The first is a paperwork relationship that delivers leads months before satellite imagery can. The second is the specifier door — the highest-probability lead source of all, opened by a favour that costs DS one visit.

The permit feed

The sourceDistrict assembly development-control offices record every approved building permit — the developer, the location, and often the architect of record — before construction visibly starts.
The relationshipOne courtesy visit to the relevant offices by Daniel, as the founder of a 26-year-old licensed company, to introduce DS and ask how permit information for the target districts can be reviewed. One follow-up call a month thereafter.
What it givesA monthly list of approved projects in the corridor districts — logged into the dossier at "permit" stage, with the architect pursued from day one. DS sees the project before the slab does.
The bonusThe same relationship doubles as tender intelligence: DS already works on government projects, and assemblies are a source of institutional work. Two engines, one visit.

The specifier door — openers that cost nothing

A
Free measure & quotation
For any architect's live project: DS measures and quotes openings at no cost. The quote becomes a document on their desk with DS's name on every page.
B
The spec library
System drawings, profiles, finishes and standard details, ready to drop into drawings. Architects specify whoever makes their work easiest.
C
The factory & showroom visit
When the Nima yard is visitor-ready, a hosted visit becomes DS's strongest proof. Until then, portfolio photos of Ashesi, UG and Toyota-grade work stand in.
D
The site board follow-up
Every architect harvested from a board gets the same treatment: portfolio, spec library, one project's free quote. Twenty years of reputation opens; this keeps it open.
Why no ads — restated. This engine competes on information, not spending: DS knows the site, the stage, the developer, the contractor and the architect before rivals have driven past. An ad buys attention once. A dossier buys a project for its whole duration.

What success looks like at 13 weeks. A dossier holding 100+ verified sites, a known cost per lead measured in fuel, one or two corridors proven and others corrected, and — the real prize — a growing set of architects who specify DS because DS showed up at their sites before anyone asked. At that point, and only then, the automation decision (section 01) is made with evidence instead of hope.
